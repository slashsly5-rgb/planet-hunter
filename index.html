        How to use

        How to use Planet Hunter

        Planet Hunter shows real pictures of the sky from NASA's SPHEREx telescope. In them the stars stay still and the worlds of our Solar System move. Here is what each part does and how to use it.

        1. The front page: our Solar System in 3D

        • Look around: drag to turn the view. Scroll, or pinch on a phone, to zoom in and out.
        • Learn: click any planet, the Sun, Saturn's rings, the asteroid belt or SPHEREx to open its info card.
        • Fly to a world: pick one from the list of worlds. The camera flies there and shows the real SPHEREx pictures of it.
        • Go bigger: the buttons under the list zoom out to where a Planet X might be, and to the whole Milky Way.
        • Time: the bar at the bottom moves the date, so you can watch the planets travel along their orbits.

        2. Explore: watch the sky change

        • Choose one of the ten worlds. Then pick a view from the buttons above the picture:
        • Three-colour: three real pictures in red, green and blue. The moving world shows up three times, once in each colour.
        • Time-lapse: plays the real pictures one after another, like a short film.
        • Blink: flips between the first and last picture. Look for the dot that jumps.
        • Difference: subtracts one picture from the other. Stars disappear; what moved is left.
        • 3D time stack: each picture becomes a sheet in a pile. Stars make straight pillars; the world makes a slanted line. Drag to turn it.
        • Computer scan: see what an automatic detector finds, and when it gets it wrong.
        • Six months apart: the same patch of sky half a year later, for worlds SPHEREx saw twice.
        • Measure it yourself: click the world in two pictures and compare your answer with NASA's.

        3. Hunt: find moving worlds like an astronomer

        • There are 12 missions. Each has up to three steps. A card on the picture tells you what to do: read it, then press its button to begin.
        • Detect: two real pictures take turns. Tap the dot that jumps while the stars stay still. You have two tries.
        • Predict: click on the picture where you think the world will be (or was) on another date. Then you see the real picture from that date.
        • Classify: from how fast it moved, choose where it orbits.
        • Press Next, or the Enter key, to move on. Stuck? Press Skip this step. At the end you get a rank and a result card to share.

        4. Planet X lab: why a far planet is so hard to find

        • The chart shows the real worlds: the farther from the Sun, the slower they move across the sky.
        • Move the slider to push a made-up planet farther away, and pick its size. Watch it become fainter and slower in two real pictures.

        5. Voyage: the space game

        • On a computer: steer with the mouse or the arrow keys. Lasers fire by themselves. Right click or X launches a missile; Space boosts.
        • On a phone: tilt the phone to steer (allow motion access if asked). Use the MISSILE and BOOST buttons, and LEVEL to reset the tilt.
        • At each world, your ship's scanner uses real SPHEREx pictures. Follow the ship computer's instructions to lock on.

        6. Handy extras

        • Words with a dotted line, such as au or arcsecond: tap them for a plain explanation.
        • BM (top right) switches the whole site to Bahasa Malaysia. ♪ turns the music on or off.
        • About explains where the pictures come from and what is real. Teachers will find a printable classroom sheet on the front page, below the 3D view.

        About

        What you are looking at

        Planet Hunter was built for the 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge, "Planet X and SPHEREx": make a public tool that shows SPHEREx images of the sky and makes it easy for anyone to see how they change over time.

        The telescope

        SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope launched in 2025. It maps the entire sky about every six months in 102 colours of near-infrared light, which our eyes cannot see. Each image here covers a patch of sky about a fifth of a degree across, smaller than half the width of the full Moon.

        How these images were made

        • For each object we asked NASA JPL Horizons where it was on each date.
        • We found the SPHEREx exposures that covered that spot in the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive.
        • We cut out the same small patch of sky from each, turned north up, and adjusted the brightness so faint things show.
        • A level is only included if the object is detected exactly where JPL says it should be.

        The orange and purple colours are false colour: brightness shown as colour. Switch it off in Explore to see the plain greyscale images. Small specks that appear in only one image are cosmic rays hitting the detector, not objects.

        In the 3D Solar System, the orbits and positions are real, from NASA JPL Horizons. The planets and worlds are drawn very much larger than life so they can be seen, and the specks of the asteroid belt are decoration. The background is a real all-sky photograph of the Milky Way. The pink ring marking where a Planet X might be is hypothetical. The Milky Way you see when you zoom far out is a model made of particles, because nobody can photograph our galaxy from outside: its arm shapes are illustrative, and the Solar System is drawn enormously too large within it.

        Three-colour pictures

        The three-colour view combines three real SPHEREx images of the same patch, taken at different times and usually at different infrared wavelengths, as red, green and blue. Longer wavelengths are shown redder. Stars are in all three images, so their colour reflects how bright they are at each wavelength. A moving world is in a different place each time, so it appears three times, once in each colour. Each image's brightness is scaled separately, so the colours are a guide, not a measurement.

        The hunt and the automatic detector

        Each hunt mission has three steps taken from real astronomy: detect the object, predict where it will be in a third real image, and classify it by how fast it moves. The last two missions are Planet X drills: a made-up planet placed into real images, always labelled as simulated.

        The "Computer scan" is classic image processing, not machine learning: it subtracts one image from the other and flags what is left. On our ten real worlds its top pick is right for seven. It is fooled by cosmic rays and it misses the two faintest, most distant worlds, which is exactly where a Planet X would be.

        Data and method

        Every picture on this site was made by the same six steps, run once with Python scripts. Nothing is changed except alignment, brightness and colour.

        1. NASA JPL HorizonsWhere each world was, at the exact moment of each image.
        2. SPHEREx archiveWhich exposures covered that spot (NASA/IPAC, releases DR1 and QR3).
        3. Cut-outRead only the small patch needed, from NASA's copy on AWS Open Data.
        4. AlignTurn north up on a common grid, so stars match pixel for pixel.
        5. CheckKeep a world only if it is detected at the predicted spot in both images, at least 5 times the background noise.
        6. Your screenSmall pictures and one data file. The site never needs the archive while you use it.

        Simplifications, stated openly

        • Positions are computed from Earth's centre. SPHEREx orbits about 7,000 km from it, which shifts the nearest worlds here by up to about 5 arcseconds: less than one detector pixel (6.2 arcseconds).
        • The Planet X simulator scales brightness and motion simply from Pluto (brightness falls with the fourth power of distance, motion with distance).
        • Classifying a world by its speed is a first clue only: speed across the sky also depends on where Earth is in its orbit.
        • Picture brightness is stretched separately in each image so faint things show. It is not a measurement.
        • The shooting stars and coloured haze on the front page, and the music, are decoration.

        What this can't do

        • It does not search for new objects. Every object here is already known, and we chose and checked each one.
        • It shows small prepared patches of sky, not the whole SPHEREx survey.
        • Brightness is adjusted so faint objects are visible. Brightness on screen is not a scientific measurement.

        What is real in the Voyage game

        The scanner pictures, positions, distances and sunlight levels are real. The ship, debris, weapons and radio voices are fiction. The worlds you fly past are 3D models wrapped in surface maps: Pluto uses NASA's New Horizons map; Uranus, Neptune and Earth use maps based on NASA imagery; Ceres, Haumea and Makemake use artists' maps, because Haumea and Makemake have never been seen up close; Vesta, Pallas, Juno and Hygiea use a map of our Moon as a stand-in, on a made-up shape. The background sky is a real all-sky photograph of the Milky Way.
