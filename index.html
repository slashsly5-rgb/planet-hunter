        About

        What you are looking at

        Planet Hunter was built for the 2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge, "Planet X and SPHEREx": make a public tool that shows SPHEREx images of the sky and makes it easy for anyone to see how they change over time.

        The telescope

        SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope launched in 2025. It maps the entire sky about every six months in 102 colours of near-infrared light, which our eyes cannot see. Each image here covers a patch of sky about a fifth of a degree across, smaller than half the width of the full Moon.

        How these images were made

        • For each object we asked NASA JPL Horizons where it was on each date.
        • We found the SPHEREx exposures that covered that spot in the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive.
        • We cut out the same small patch of sky from each, turned north up, and adjusted the brightness so faint things show.
        • A level is only included if the object is detected exactly where JPL says it should be.

        The orange and purple colours are false colour: brightness shown as colour. Switch it off in Explore to see the plain greyscale images. Small specks that appear in only one image are cosmic rays hitting the detector, not objects.

        In the 3D Solar System, the orbits and positions are real, from NASA JPL Horizons. The planets and worlds are drawn very much larger than life so they can be seen, and the specks of the asteroid belt are decoration. The background is a real all-sky photograph of the Milky Way. The pink ring marking where a Planet X might be is hypothetical.

        Three-colour pictures

        The three-colour view combines three real SPHEREx images of the same patch, taken at different times and usually at different infrared wavelengths, as red, green and blue. Longer wavelengths are shown redder. Stars are in all three images, so their colour reflects how bright they are at each wavelength. A moving world is in a different place each time, so it appears three times, once in each colour. Each image's brightness is scaled separately, so the colours are a guide, not a measurement.

        The hunt and the automatic detector

        Each hunt mission has three steps taken from real astronomy: detect the object, predict where it will be in a third real image, and classify it by how fast it moves. The last two missions are Planet X drills: a made-up planet placed into real images, always labelled as simulated.

        The "Computer scan" is classic image processing, not machine learning: it subtracts one image from the other and flags what is left. On our ten real worlds its top pick is right for seven. It is fooled by cosmic rays and it misses the two faintest, most distant worlds, which is exactly where a Planet X would be.

        What this can't do

        • It does not search for new objects. Every object here is already known, and we chose and checked each one.
        • It shows small prepared patches of sky, not the whole SPHEREx survey.
        • Brightness is adjusted so faint objects are visible. Brightness on screen is not a scientific measurement.

        What is real in the Voyage game

        The scanner pictures, positions, distances and sunlight levels are real. The ship, debris, weapons and radio voices are fiction. The worlds you fly past are 3D models wrapped in surface maps: Pluto uses NASA's New Horizons map; Uranus, Neptune and Earth use maps based on NASA imagery; Ceres, Haumea and Makemake use artists' maps, because Haumea and Makemake have never been seen up close; Vesta, Pallas, Juno and Hygiea use a map of our Moon as a stand-in, on a made-up shape. The background sky is a real all-sky photograph of the Milky Way.
